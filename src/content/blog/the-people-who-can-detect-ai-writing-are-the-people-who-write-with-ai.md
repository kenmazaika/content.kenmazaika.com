**TL;DR**

- **What it's about:** every agent content pipeline runs a humanize stage on the fear that readers will detect the AI and bounce — but the detection record splits the other way: lay readers judge at coin-flip (56.7% correct on AI text, and they called 51.7% of human text AI), OpenAI's own detector was 26% accurate and got retired, and the only reliable detectors are people who write with LLMs daily (92.7% true positives, holding even against "humanized" text).
- **What to do differently:** decide who you're hiding from before you spend a pass — for general audiences, skip the camouflage and spend on substance; for LLM-dense communities (agent-tool subreddits, X agent circles, technical cold-email lists), scrub the three signals expert readers actually use: AI-vocabulary tells, generic formality and structure, and missing originality, with receipts as the highest-leverage edit. Delete detector-score gates from the pipeline.
- **The key takeaway:** AI-text detection is a specialist skill, and the specialists are people who write with AI — which makes your target audience the one group that can tell. They decide from vocabulary, structure, and originality, not from anything a detector measures.

This afternoon I asked my agent to make two posts sound less like it wrote them. The full-length version was already cut to a takeaway list, then grown to a four-hundred-word story, and the last request was the same one I made a week ago to five parallel batches of cold-email hooks: humanize it, make it sound like a person. The fear under all of it is that a reader will clock the text as AI and bounce. So I went looking for the evidence on who can actually do that. The record does not split the way the fear does.

Start with the tools, because they're the easy fear to retire. OpenAI shipped a text classifier in January 2023 that, by its own evaluation, correctly identified 26% of AI-written text — and mislabeled 9% of human text — before the company [retired it six months later](https://openai.com/index/new-ai-classifier-for-indicating-ai-written-text/) "due to its low rate of accuracy." The independent record is worse: when Stanford ran seven commercial detectors over real human essays ([Liang et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819)), the average false-positive rate on essays by non-native English speakers was 61.3%, and 97.8% of those human essays were flagged as AI by at least one detector. ChatGPT-"enhanced" vocabulary cut that rate by fifty points — the detectors reward fancy wording, not humanness. And the platforms you'd actually care about never gated on provenance anyway: Google's guidance has said since 2023 that it rewards [quality of content, not how it was produced](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2023/02/google-search-and-ai-content), and its spam policy targets scaled volume and manipulation, not AI use. A detector gate in your pipeline is a gate on a known failure.

The readers are the real test, and they split by class. In the largest recent study ([Russell et al., ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267/)), annotators read 300 nonfiction articles written by humans or by GPT-4o, Claude, and o1. The annotators who rarely use LLMs for writing performed at chance: they correctly flagged 56.7% of AI articles and called 51.7% of human articles AI — a coin flip they reported at 4.03 out of 5 confidence. The annotators who write with LLMs daily were a different population: 92.7% true positives, and a majority vote of five of them misclassified one article in 300, outperforming most commercial detectors even when the AI text had been paraphrased or run through humanizers. Laypeople mistake both directions: in a preregistered Turing-test study ([Jones & Bergen, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.08007)), GPT-4 was judged human 54% of the time, while actual humans were judged human only 67% — a third of real people read as AI.

Here is the part that should change your pipeline. The people who can detect AI text are the people who write with AI — and those are the people you are writing for. An agent-content blog, an agent-tool subreddit, the X circles that post about harnesses and workflows: the readership is daily LLM users, and even a cold-email list of technical founders leans that way. That is an extrapolation from the study, not a measured fact about any community — but it is the direction every signal points. "Humanize it" was right. The question was always which reader you were hiding from, and the answer is the one reader who can actually tell.

What that reader uses is the actionable part, because the same study logged it. The experts' explanations leaned on lexical clues — an "AI vocabulary" they could name — and beyond that, on formality, structure, and originality: whether the piece had a real point of view and specifics that couldn't have been generated. That is a three-item checklist, not a vibe. Scrub the vocabulary tells, break the too-formal structure, and — the highest-leverage edit — put the receipts in. Originality is the signal experts weight beyond vocabulary, the hardest one to fake, and the same feature that makes a piece worth reading. It is also what generic friendliness tends to strip out. I keep a half-dial rule against over-humanizing agent output — the "maybe just a bit too messy" version of a text reads as human to nobody. A real number or a dated detail out-detects a typo every time.

So the decision is a two-tier rule. Know which class your reader is in before you spend a pass. Writing for the general internet: stop scrubbing, start adding — the camouflage is invisible to that audience and the substance isn't. Writing for people who run agents, or for technical buyers who live in LLMs: scrub the three signals, keep the receipts, and never run your draft through a detector to check your work. The people who can catch you don't need a tool, and the tools can't catch you anyway.
