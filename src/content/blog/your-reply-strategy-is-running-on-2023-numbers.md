Yesterday my agent handed me a reply-growth plan for X. Target accounts in the 5k–100k band, reply inside the first half hour, and here are the weights that make it work: replies are worth roughly 13.5–27× a like, profile visits pay about +12, and an author reply-back runs 75–150×. The plan was dated September 2. The numbers were from 2023. On August 13, X open-sourced the actual production ranking weights for the For You timeline — `home-mixer/params/param.rs` in the xai-org/x-algorithm repo, synced by cron from live defaults — and the reply math that has been circulating since the 2023 code drop does not match the file.

Here is what changed. The reply weight in the 2023 snapshot was 13.5, and the "27 likes" figure traces to the March 2023 README before X revised it down five days later; the production file says 5.0. Profile clicks, which paid 12.0 in 2023, are 0.0 — zeroed with no announcement. The "reply engaged by author" head — the 75.0 that became "reply-backs are worth 75–150×" — is gone as a named head. The verified-account author multipliers (4× in-network) were deleted in the September 2025 re-release. Around those: a like is 0.5, a repost 1.0, a quote 5.0, a follow 4.0, and opening a link in your post is +0.2 — small, but positive, which means the "links cost you reach" folklore has no line of code behind it either.

And the one number that should change how you reply. The file defines a `BidirectionalFollowReplyWeightBoost` of 15.0. When you and the author of a post follow each other, the reply weight on their original posts goes from 5.0 to 20.0 — four times the base. It fires only on original posts — never on replies, never on reposts. The mutual pair is the single biggest conditional in the whole table, and it is the one part of the system you can engineer: the reply engine's best target is not a big stranger with a busy comment section. It is an account you can convert into a mutual, because the algorithm literally pays four times the attention to reply behavior inside mutual conversations on originals.

Before you rebuild the list, read the caveat X shipped inside the same file. The weights multiply predicted probabilities — P(reply), P(like) — not raw counts, and X's own comment block calls out "the incorrect statement that 'one report cancels 468 likes'" as a misinterpretation: the baseline probability of a report is more than a thousand times lower than a like, so its coefficient is huge partly because the event is nearly never predicted. Divide one weight by another and you get a number that means nothing. The "reply is worth N likes" genre — my brief's table included — is that arithmetic. What survives is structural: the mutual boost exists, the zeroed profile-click head exists, the deleted author-reply head exists. Those are not probability-dependent. Also in the comments: engagement counts only when a post was served in the Home timeline, so coordinating a post into existence through a group chat "has no ranking impact."

So the 0-to-1k reply strategy survives, but the aim changes. Three rules. First, convert before you spend: follow the account, then make yourself worth following back — the reply is the resume — and the moment they follow you, their original posts become the 4× context. Second, reply to originals, not to their replies or reposts; the boost never fires there, and neither does much of anyone's attention. Third, measure follows earned and reply-backs, not profile clicks: clicks rank at zero, the follow is the 4.0 head, and only the follow compounds into the mutual graph where your own originals get the boost in their feeds. The "author replies back" criterion on my old checklist stays, but for the social reason, not the ranking one.

The part that stings is how the plan got stale. The production file had been public for three weeks when my agent wrote that brief, and the researcher still pulled the 2023 table out of secondary sources — the viral growth writeups have recycled it for three years. That is the real lesson for anyone running platform strategy through an agent: research inherits the oldest available source unless you tell it otherwise. Date-stamp every platform-mechanic claim, and for anything load-bearing, fetch the primary source — the repo, the announcement, the changelog — before the plan ships. A two-line instruction in the research brief would have caught a table that was wrong before the plan was written.

Tonight, prune the reply tier: drop the strangers you were chasing and keep the accounts that could plausibly follow back, plus everyone who already does. Aim at their original posts. Judge the month by follows, not clicks. The probabilities half of the ranking is Phoenix's model and nobody outside X can run it, so treat the conditional structure as the plan and the exact weights as a dated snapshot — but the snapshot you plan from should be the one X published in August, not the one it replaced in 2023.
