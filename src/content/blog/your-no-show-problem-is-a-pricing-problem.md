**TL;DR**

- **What it's about:** I'm selling a 25-seat live workshop at $97 off a cold-email list, and until this week I had the price filed under "friction." The attendance data points the other way: organizers report no-show rates as high as 50% for free events, and about 20% even when people prepay.
- **What to do differently:** charge for the seat and never comp it, and attach a commitment device — a deposit returned only when they show, or a small no-show penalty. Plan the room on the show rate, not the registration count, and keep the reminders without treating them as the lever.
- **The key takeaway:** a free registration is an option, not a commitment. Present bias makes people skip what costs them nothing, so the price buys attendance, not just revenue.

Matt Juszczak opened Turnstyle Cycle's first indoor cycling studio in Boston's Back Bay and watched 40 to 50 people a day reserve a bike and never ride it. Over a year that's more than 15,000 booked-and-abandoned slots, in a room with a fixed number of bikes. His summary of the early days: "It was a disaster."

That story sits in a 2019 paper from Harvard and Technion researchers on what they call the underutilization of reserved resources ([Ma, Meir, Parkes, and Wu-Yan](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.09713)). The examples are not exotic: skipped exercise classes, last-minute dentist cancellations, squash courts at the Harvard gym — and event registrations. Same problem, different room.

**The number is the same wherever registration is free**

The paper gathers the practitioner data alongside the models. Organizers of free events told Eventbrite their no-show rate can run as high as 50%. Even for prepaid events run through Doorkeeper, no-shows still reach 20%. Free flips half the room; charging roughly halves the loss.

Two things are true in that pair, and only one is obvious. The obvious one: people who pay show up more. The non-obvious one: paid registrants still no-show at 20%, so unless you build the room on the number who actually arrive, you have planned for people who are not coming.

**Why the price works**

The mechanism is present bias. The person who reserves a seat a week out and the person who has to show up on the day are not the same decision-maker, and the one who has to get dressed usually loses. The paper's fix is a commitment device: a contingent payment. Participants bid the no-show penalty they are willing to accept, the scarce seats go to the people most likely to use them, and the simulation beats the mechanisms organizers actually use. If you have ever taken a deposit you only return on attendance, you built a crude version of this by hand.

**Reminders help, but they are a rounding error next to the price**

It is tempting to treat attendance as a copywriting problem: more reminders, better reminders, a text an hour before. Reminders do work. Kaiser Permanente randomized 158,669 appointments and found that adding a second text reminder cut no-shows 7% in primary care and 11% in mental health ([The Permanente Journal, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9126539/)). Real cuts, and you should take them.

Then compare the size of the two levers. A second reminder buys you roughly a tenth of your no-shows. Charging the seat is the difference between a 50% hole and a 20% one. Same week of work, wildly different payoff — and the reminder is the one that feels like progress, because it is the one you can write.

**What to do differently**

- **Never comp a seat.** A comped seat is a free registration with your logo on it: same option, same present bias, same half-empty room on the day. If you want someone there, sell them the seat and let them decide it is worth showing up for.
- **Attach a commitment device.** A deposit returned only on attendance, or a small no-show penalty, turns an option into a plan. It is the paper's mechanism with a checkout page standing in for the bid.
- **Plan the room on the show rate.** A 25-seat room means 25 paid seats and about 20 people. Fill it with free registrations and you should plan on half. The cap only means what the price makes it mean.
- **Keep the reminders; stop rewriting them.** The calendar invite, the 48-hour note, the day-of text — set them up once. The hour you spend polishing reminder copy is the lowest-leverage hour in the launch.

None of this is an argument for charging more. It is an argument for charging something. Free is not a lower price; it is a different product, one where the seat is only a maybe and the room you promised is the room you are guessing at.

So the next time you build a registration page, pick which job you are doing: filling a list or filling a room. If it is the room, put a price on the seat, and give people a reason to lose it.
